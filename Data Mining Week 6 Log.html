The 7-step recursive tree induction algorithm

Decision tree induction follows a systematic top-down recursive divide-and-conquer strategy:

  1. 00:55:201. IngestPlace all labeled training samples at root node.
  2. 00:55:452. GenerateGenerate candidate feature and threshold splits.
  3. 00:56:103. MeasureMeasure impurity of resulting child partitions.
  4. 00:56:404. SelectChoose candidate split yielding greatest impurity drop.
  5. 00:57:155. DispatchPartition dataset and send subsets to child branches.
  6. 00:57:506. RecurseRecursively repeat algorithm on non-terminal children.
  7. 00:58:307. HaltAssign majority class to leaves when stopping rule met.

Pure vs Mixed Nodes: A Pure Node contains samples belonging strictly to one class (e.g. 8 YES, 0 NO). In a pure node, uncertainty is zero. A Mixed Node contains multiple classes and requires further partitioning.

01:13:21 to 01:17:30Slides 16–18

Shannon Entropy: Measuring disorder and untidiness

Dr. Bulut explains that in data mining, entropy measures the disorder, untidiness, or uncertainty of class labels within a subset. For a node \(S\) with \(c\) target classes, Shannon Entropy (measured in bits) is defined as:

$$H(S) = - \sum_{i=1}^{c} p_i \log_2(p_i)$$

For a binary classification task with positive class proportion \(p_+\) and negative class proportion \(p_-\):

$$H(S) = - \left[ p_+ \log_2(p_+) + p_- \log_2(p_-) \right]$$

where \(p_i\) is the proportion of class \(i\). Crucial mathematical convention: \(0 \cdot \log_2(0) \equiv 0\). For balanced classes, entropy scales monotonically with class count:

Number of ClassesDistribution (100 Items Total)Formula EvaluationShannon Entropy
1 Class100 Red\(- [1.0 \cdot \log_2(1.0)] = 0\)0.000 bits (Pure)
2 Classes50 Red, 50 White\(- [0.5(-1) + 0.5(-1)]\)1.000 bit (Max 2-class)
3 Classes34 Red, 33 White, 33 Green\(\log_2(3)\)1.585 bits
4 Classes25 Red, 25 White, 25 Green, 25 Blue\(\log_2(4)\)2.000 bits
5 Classes20 of each colour\(\log_2(5)\)2.322 bits

The Binary Curve: Plotted against \(p\), entropy forms a concave parabola peaking at \(p = 0.5\) with \(H = 1.0\), and dropping to \(0\) at both \(p = 0.0\) and \(p = 1.0\).

Worked hand calculations & Slide 65 Question 1

Dr. Bulut works through three concrete numerical scenarios that frequently recur on examinations:

01:15:24Pure Node (8 YES, 0 NO)\(H = - [1.0 \log_2(1.0) + 0] = \mathbf{0.000}\text{ bits}\)
01:16:1350/50 Split (4 YES, 4 NO)\(H = - [0.5(-1) + 0.5(-1)] = \mathbf{1.000}\text{ bit}\)
EXAM RADAR · SLIDE 65 QUESTION 1 Slide 21 · Slide 65

Problem: A node contains 6 YES and 2 NO instances. Calculate its Shannon Entropy.

Step 1: Class proportions
\(p_+ = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75, \quad p_- = \frac{2}{8} = 0.25\)

Step 2: Base-2 logarithms
\(\log_2(0.25) = -2.000, \quad \log_2(0.75) = \log_2(3/4) \approx 1.585 - 2.0 = -0.415\)

Step 3: Multiply terms
\(0.75 \times (-0.415) \approx -0.3113, \quad 0.25 \times (-2.0) = -0.5000\)

Step 4: Sum and negate
$$H(S) = - (-0.3113 - 0.5000) = \mathbf{0.8113} \approx \mathbf{0.811}\text{ bits}$$

01:21:40 to 01:23:10Slides 23–25

Information Gain (IG) & The Inverse Entropy Fallacy

Information Gain (IG) measures the expected reduction in entropy resulting from splitting dataset \(S\) on attribute \(A\):

$$IG(S, A) = H(S) - \sum_{v \in \text{Values}(A)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} H(S_v)$$

EXAM RADAR · FALLACY WARNING 01:20:10 · Slide 23

The Inverse Entropy Fallacy: Dr. Bulut warned that students sometimes mistake Information Gain for the mathematical inverse of entropy (\(1/H\)). Information Gain is NOT \(1/H\). It is the subtraction difference between parent entropy and weighted child entropy: \(IG = H(\text{parent}) - H(\text{children})\).

Sample Weighting: Child entropies must be weighted by \(\frac{|S_v|}{|S|}\). A child node containing 90% of the dataset must heavily influence the split evaluation, whereas a tiny child containing only 10% must not distort the global split score.

01:23:10 to 01:24:28Slides 26–30

Candidate splits comparison & ID3 Greedy Learning

Consider a parent node with 8 samples (4 YES, 4 NO) where parent entropy \(H(S) = 1.000\) bit (Slide 27):

Candidate SplitLeft Child SubsetRight Child SubsetWeighted Child EntropyInformation Gain (IG)
Candidate A3 YES, 1 NO (\(H \approx 0.811\))1 YES, 3 NO (\(H \approx 0.811\))\(\frac{4}{8}(0.811) + \frac{4}{8}(0.811) = 0.811\)\(1.000 - 0.811 = \mathbf{0.189}\text{ bits}\)
Candidate B (Perfect)4 YES, 0 NO (\(H = 0.000\))0 YES, 4 NO (\(H = 0.000\))\(\frac{4}{8}(0) + \frac{4}{8}(0) = 0.000\)\(1.000 - 0.000 = \mathbf{1.000}\text{ bit}\)

Candidate B achieves \(IG = 1.000\) bit and is chosen as the root split. Quinlan’s ID3 algorithm repeats this evaluation greedily at every step. Greedy search does not guarantee a globally optimal tree (which is NP-complete), but provides a fast, computationally tractable polynomial-time approximation.

01:24:28 to 01:27:14Slides 31–35

Gini Impurity & Slide 65 Question 2

Gini Impurity measures the probability of misclassifying a randomly chosen element if labeled according to class distribution. It relies on squaring probabilities rather than computing logarithms:

$$\text{Gini}(S) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{c} p_i^2$$

For a candidate split on attribute \(A\), the weighted child Gini impurity is:

$$\text{Gini}_{\text{split}}(S, A) = \sum_{v \in \text{Values}(A)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} \text{Gini}(S_v)$$

EXAM RADAR · SLIDE 65 QUESTION 2 Slide 33 · Slide 65

Problem: A node has 75% Class A and 25% Class B. Calculate its Gini Impurity.

Step 1: Square class proportions
\(p_A^2 = (0.75)^2 = 0.5625, \quad p_B^2 = (0.25)^2 = 0.0625\)

Step 2: Sum squared probabilities
\(\sum_{i=1}^{c} p_i^2 = 0.5625 + 0.0625 = 0.6250\)

Step 3: Subtract from 1
$$\text{Gini}(S) = 1 - 0.6250 = \mathbf{0.375}$$

01:27:14 to 01:30:45Slides 36–41

CART & The “2-in-1 Shampoo” hybrid model

Developed by Leo Breiman in 1984, CART strictly produces binary trees (exactly two child branches per internal node). CART carves feature space into piecewise axis-aligned hyper-rectangles using horizontal and vertical planes.

INSTRUCTOR INSIGHT · HYBRID CART 01:26:56 – 01:28:45

The “2-in-1 Shampoo” Analogy: Dr. Bulut compared modern CART architectures to 2-in-1 shampoo (cleaner + polisher in one bottle). CART combines a decision tree router with leaf regressors: the tree hierarchically partitions the problem space into distinct regimes, and within each leaf, a dedicated linear regression equation (\(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\)) or local \(k\)-NN model generates continuous predictions.

Tree Depth, Overfitting & Pruning

Model capacity, decision stumps, the 100% training trap, noise fitting, and pre-pruning vs cost-complexity pruning.

01:30:45 to 01:35:40Slides 42–47

Tree depth & The 100% training accuracy trap

Tree depth is the length of the longest path from the root to any leaf node. Shallow trees (Depth = 1, decision stumps) underfit. Deep trees (Depth ≥ 8) overfit by memorizing accidental noise and single outliers.

EXAM RADAR · SLIDE 65 QUESTION 3 01:37:30 · Slide 47 · Slide 65

Question: A decision tree has 100% training accuracy but substantially worse accuracy on unseen data. What is the likely problem?

Answer: OVERFITTING (or OVERLEARNING). 100% training accuracy is a diagnostic symptom of pathology: the tree fragmented its leaves down to single training samples, memorizing measurement noise and errors rather than genuine population patterns.

Tree ArchitectureTraining AccuracyTest Accuracy (Unseen)Diagnostic Status
Unconstrained Deep Tree100.0%72.0%Severe Overfitting (Noise memorised)
Pruned Balanced Tree96.0%87.0%Optimal Fit (+15% test generalisation boost)

At 01:33:30, Dr. Bulut cites a traditional folk proverb: an over-analyzer examines every trivial detail and becomes paralyzed, whereas a pragmatic thinker avoids drowning in details and moves forward. Over-learned trees suffer the same fate when exposed to real-world noise.

01:35:40 to 01:38:23Slides 48–50

Pruning strategies (Budama): Pre-pruning vs Post-pruning

Pruning (budama) removes unnecessary branches and leaves to reduce variance, improve test generalisation, and restore interpretability.

EXAM RADAR · SLIDE 65 QUESTION 4 Slide 48 · Slide 50 · Slide 65

Question: What can we do when a Decision Tree is overfitting?

Answer: PRUNE THE TREE AND REGULARIZE COMPLEXITY.

Pre-Pruning (Early Stopping)

  • 01:36:50Halts growth early during recursive construction.
  • 01:37:05Hyperparameters: max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf.
  • 01:37:15Fast, but risks premature stopping before finding multi-feature synergies (horizon effect).

Post-Pruning (Cost-Complexity)

  • 01:37:30Grows full overfitted tree \(T_0\) first, then prunes backwards.
  • 01:38:00Minimizes cost-complexity objective:

    $$R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha |T|$$

    where \(R(T)\) is misclassification error, \(|T|\) is terminal leaf count, and \(\alpha \ge 0\).
  • 01:38:05Tuning \(\alpha\) on validation data systematically collapses weak leaves without horizon risk.

Alternative Classifiers & Interview Defense

Classifier robustness, Naïve Bayes probability models, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and technical interview questions.

01:38:23 to 01:40:50Slides 51–58

Classifier robustness & Probabilistic Naïve Bayes

Robustness (gürbüzlük) measures the stability of decision boundaries when small sample variations or noise occur. Decision trees can be unstable because a minor shift at the root cascades down the entire hierarchy. Furthermore, orthogonal axis-aligned splits struggle to approximate smooth diagonal boundaries, requiring dozens of stair-step partitions.

Slide 53 Mini-Exercise: When comparing Split A (3/2 vs 2/3, \(IG \approx 0.029\)) against Split B (5/0 vs 0/5, \(IG = 1.000\)), Split B is unambiguously superior because it achieves 100% pure terminal subsets.

Naïve Bayes (Slides 54–58): A supervised probabilistic classifier based on Bayes' Rule:

$$P(C \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto P(C) \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid C)$$

It is called “Naïve” because it assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class label. In email spam filtering, word likelihoods (e.g. \(P(\text{FREE} \mid \text{Spam}) = 0.85\)) are multiplied with the prior spam rate to predict classification.

01:40:50 to 01:43:17Slides 59–68

Support Vector Machines & Technical Interview Defense

A Support Vector Machine (SVM) finds a linear hyperplane that separates classes while maximizing the geometric margin:

$$\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b = 0 \quad \text{with Margin } M = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

The critical training instances lying directly on the margin planes (\(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i + b = \pm 1\)) are called Support Vectors. Non-support vectors can be moved or deleted without altering the decision boundary. For non-linear data, the kernel trick implicitly projects points into a high-dimensional space.

JOB INTERVIEW DEFENSE · 15-MINUTE PROMPTS 01:41:30

Dr. Bulut's Technical Interview Prompts: In his closing remarks, Dr. Bulut warned that ML engineering interviews routinely evaluate candidates on two defense questions:

1. Why does overfitting occur in decision trees? Defense: Leaf fragmentation from exponential subset decay; fitting 5% natural noise with dedicated branches; unlimited non-parametric capacity acting as a lookup table.
2. How do you eliminate overfitting in production? Defense: Enforce pre-pruning constraints (max_depth, min_samples_leaf); apply cost-complexity post-pruning (\(\alpha\)); deploy Random Forests to average out variance across decorrelated bootstrap trees.
