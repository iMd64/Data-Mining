Classifiers vs. Regressors and Learning Paradigms

Discrete boundaries vs continuous lines, learning from labeled ground truth, unsupervised clustering, and the 1,000 animal images dilemma.

00:15:00to 00:24:40Slides 11–14

Classifier vs. Regressor

Dr. Bulut emphasizes the fundamental distinction in supervised learning: classifiers predict qualitative categories, whereas regressors predict quantitative numbers. A classifier maps feature vectors \(X\) into a finite set of discrete labels \(y \in \{C_1, C_2, \dots, C_k\}\). A regressor maps feature vectors \(X\) into an infinite continuous real-valued space \(y \in \mathbb{R}\).

Classifier

  • 00:19:10Predicts Loan Decision: Approve / Reject.
  • 00:19:40Evaluated via Accuracy, Confusion Matrix, F1-Score.

Regressor

  • 00:20:15Predicts Blood Pressure: 124.6 mmHg (Continuous).
  • 00:20:50Predicts House Price: $385,000.
  • 00:21:20Evaluated via Mean Squared Error (MSE), \(R^2\).
00:24:40to 00:36:30Slides 15–20

Supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised paradigms

How does the algorithm learn? In Supervised Learning, every training instance is accompanied by an explicit supervisor: a ground-truth class label or numerical target \(y\). The algorithm’s task is to learn a mapping function \(f(X) \approx y\).

In Unsupervised Learning, no target labels exist. The algorithm inspects the intrinsic geometric structure, density, and feature correlations of the data to discover clusters or association rules. In Semi-Supervised Learning, a small fraction of data (e.g. 5–10%) is labeled by expensive human annotators, while a massive pool (90–95%) remains unlabeled. The model uses the labeled points as anchor seeds and leverages the unlabeled distribution to refine decision boundaries.

Lazy vs. Eager Learners and the Two Phases

When does the real computation happen? Fast training vs fast inference, instance-based memory, and the lifecycle of model deployment.

00:36:30to 00:46:00Slides 21–25

Lazy vs. Eager learners: when computation happens

Dr. Bulut asks: “When does the real work happen?” Machine learning algorithms are split into two computational philosophies:

Eager learners

00:48:41 Learn first → predict instantly. Eager algorithms (Decision Trees, Naive Bayes, Neural Networks) invest heavy computational effort during training to abstract away data and compile a compact mathematical hypothesis. At test time, classifying a new query takes fractions of a millisecond: \(O(1)\) or \(O(\text{tree depth})\).

Lazy learners

00:49:15 Store examples → compute on query. Lazy algorithms (K-Nearest Neighbors, instance-based reasoning) do essentially zero computation during training—they simply store raw records in memory. But when a query arrives, they must compute distances against all stored points. Training is instantaneous, but prediction is computationally slow.

Eager StrategyHeavy Upfront Training

00:51:20 Compiles explicit mathematical model; lightweight and lightning-fast in mobile/embedded deployment.

Lazy StrategyZero Upfront Training

00:52:45 Stores raw instances; delays all Euclidean distance math until query time; memory heavy.

00:46:00to 00:54:00Slides 26–29

The two distinct phases: training vs. prediction

Students must never conflate the two stages of supervised learning. In the Training Phase, known instances \((X_{\text{train}}, y_{\text{train}})\) are passed to an inductive algorithm to establish parameters, weights, or decision splits. In the Prediction Phase, the trained model receives fresh, previously unseen instances \(X_{\text{test}}\) with zero ground-truth labels and infers their probable class \(\hat{y}\).

  2. 00:53:25Training PhaseLearn rule parameters
  3. 00:53:40Model / HypothesisStored mathematical logic
  4. 00:53:55Prediction PhaseInfer unseen queries

Decision Boundaries in 2D, 3D, and Hyperplanes

The geometry of classification, lines, planes, hyperplanes, linear simplicity versus non-linear flexibility, and confidence near the border.

00:54:00to 01:04:00Slides 30–32

The concept of a decision boundary

Dr. Bulut asks: “Where does one class end and another begin?” In feature space, a decision boundary is the geometric frontier partitioning instances of differing classes. In a 2D space (with two predictive features, \(x_1\) and \(x_2\)), the boundary is a line or curve separating points.

He illustrates a new query point landing millimeters away from the red separating boundary: when points sit deep inside a class cluster, classification confidence is near 100%; when points hover right along the boundary, confidence drops sharply and model sensitivity is acute.

01:04:00to 01:14:00Slides 33–36

Linear boundaries vs. non-linear manifolds

In two dimensions, a linear boundary is a straight line (\(w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b = 0\)). In three dimensions, it becomes a flat 2D plane cutting through a 3D cube. In an \(n\)-dimensional feature space with dozens of attributes, it is formally termed a hyperplane of dimension \(n-1\).

However, real-world data is frequently not linearly separable. If blue points surround orange points in concentric circles, a straight line cannot separate them without massive error. A non-linear boundary bends and contours to isolate complex topological regions.

Linear decision boundary

01:12:53 Straight line in 2D, flat plane in 3D, hyperplane in higher dimensions. Computationally efficient, mathematically stable, highly resistant to overfitting, but fails on complex XOR distributions.

Non-linear decision boundary

01:13:03 Curves, polynomials, or enclosed islands. Captures intricate interactions, but risks bending around random noise if unconstrained (overfitting).

White-Box Transparency vs. Black-Box Power

The interpretability spectrum, human-readable logic for medicine and law, and the opaque representation of deep neural networks.

01:14:00to 01:26:00Slides 37–40

The interpretability spectrum

Dr. Bulut presents one of the most critical dilemmas in modern artificial intelligence: how easily can a human comprehend the model’s reasoning?

A White-Box Model (like a shallow Decision Tree or a set of IF-THEN rules) offers complete transparency. A physician or judge can follow every single branch: “If Age < 30 and Income > $50,000 → Approve Loan.” In regulated healthcare and finance, transparency is legally mandatory under right-to-explanation directives.

In contrast, a Black-Box Model (such as a Deep Convolutional Neural Network or an ensemble of 500 Random Forest trees) projects data across thousands of non-linear matrix multiplications. While its predictive accuracy may be stellar, its internal decision reasoning is completely inaccessible to direct human inspection.

The Baseline Benchmark: Zero Rule (ZeroR)

Why every machine learning project needs a baseline, the ZeroR majority voting mechanism, and the deceptive illusion of high accuracy.

01:26:00to 01:34:00Slides 41–46

Zero Rule (ZeroR / 0R)

Dr. Bulut emphasizes: “Before celebrating a model, beat a simple baseline.” Beginners often train a complex model, see 72% accuracy, and assume their architecture is brilliant. But what if the simplest possible baseline achieves 70% accuracy without even looking at the features?

ZeroR (0-Rule) is the absolute simplest classification benchmark: it completely ignores all input features and unconditionally predicts the majority class observed in the training data for every single instance.

01:27:5572%fancy model accuracyTrained model
01:28:4770%ZeroR baseline accuracyZero features
01:30:11+2%marginal real gainTrue value

Consider a veterinary dataset of 100 animal cases: 70 Dogs and 30 Cats. ZeroR simply outputs “Dog” every time. It achieves 70% accuracy without looking at fur color, weight, or barking sounds! If your 50-layer deep neural network achieves 72%, it only added 2% genuine predictive value over a blind guess. Always establish the ZeroR baseline first.

Can a single attribute make a surprisingly accurate classifier? The OneR frequency table algorithm and the student passing exam benchmark.

01:34:00to 01:44:00Slides 47–55

The One Rule (OneR / 1R) algorithm

Can we improve on ZeroR by inspecting just one single attribute? That is the core idea of OneR (1-Rule), invented by Robert Holte. OneR frequently achieves 80–90% of the accuracy of complex decision trees using only the single most informative feature.

The OneR algorithm operates in three straightforward steps:

  2. 01:36:202. Form Local RulesPredict majority class per branch
  3. 01:37:403. Count ErrorsSum errors across branches
  4. 01:39:104. Select WinnerPick feature with lowest total error

Resampling, Bootstrap, and Quantization

Mathematical linear models, managing imbalanced classes via undersampling/oversampling, bootstrap replacement, and quantization versus normalization.

01:44:00to 01:49:30Slides 56–63

Linear combinations and resampling methods

Dr. Bulut illustrates the general formulation of a linear classification model: \(\text{score} = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_nx_n + b\). Each input feature is scaled by an importance weight \(w_i\), offset by a bias term \(b\).

He returns to the challenge of class imbalance: when a dataset has 90 Class A instances and 10 Class B instances, standard models become heavily biased toward A. To correct this, engineers use Resampling:

Undersampling

01:47:15 Randomly discard majority Class A instances down to 20 or 10, leveling the playing field. Risk: loses potentially valuable data.

Oversampling

01:48:00 Duplicate or synthetically generate (SMOTE) minority Class B instances up to 90. Risk: increases training time and may cause overfitting on replicated points.

He introduces Bootstrap Sampling: sampling \(n\) instances from a dataset with replacement. A single instance may be chosen twice or thrice in one sample, while ~36.8% of instances are omitted (out-of-bag). This is the foundational mechanism behind ensemble methods like Bagging and Random Forests.

01:49:30to 01:55:40Slides 64–71

Quantization vs. Normalization

Dr. Bulut warns students never to confuse Quantization with Normalization:

Normalization (Week 3)

01:50:40 Rescaling numbers. Transforms numerical values into \([0, 1]\) or \(Z\)-scores while strictly preserving continuous resolution and proportional ratios.

Quantization (Week 4)

01:51:19 Grouping numbers into bins. Replaces continuous ranges with a few discrete ordinal buckets (e.g. Exam score 0–39 → Low, 40–69 → Medium, 70–100 → High).
