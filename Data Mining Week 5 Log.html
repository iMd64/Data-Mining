The Geometry of Distance and Euclidean Metric

Distance as mathematical dissimilarity, Pythagoras in feature space, straight-line distance, and multi-dimensional coordinate separation.

00:00:00 to 00:16:11Slides 1–7

Distance equals dissimilarity

Dr. Faruk Bulut opens Week 5 by connecting attribute values to physical geometry. In data mining, distance is the formal mathematical representation of dissimilarity. When two instances sit close together in feature space, they share similar traits; when they are separated by large distances, they behave dissimilarly.

He poses the core question with two points \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (7, 6)\): how far apart are they? The answer depends entirely on the geometric metric chosen to measure the space.

00:16:11 to 00:20:22Slides 8–12

The Euclidean metric (\(L_2\) Norm)

The most familiar metric is Euclidean Distance—the ordinary straight-line distance between two points, derived directly from the Pythagorean theorem:

$$d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}$$

In two dimensions, for \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (5, 7)\):

$$d(A, B) = \sqrt{(5 - 2)^2 + (7 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5.0$$

He extends the formula to 3D feature spaces: \(A = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(B = (4, 6, 3) \implies d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = 5.0\). Euclidean distance represents the ruler distance across continuous space.

Manhattan, City-Blocks, and Grid Navigation

Moving along orthogonal grids, city block taxi navigation, L1 norm calculations, and comparing straight-line vs perimeter paths.

00:20:22 to 00:25:20Slides 13–15

Manhattan distance (\(L_1\) Norm)

If you are a taxi driver in Manhattan, you cannot drive through skyscrapers along a straight diagonal line. You must travel strictly along the horizontal streets and vertical avenues. This is Manhattan Distance (also known as City-Block or Taxicab distance):

$$d(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|$$

For the same two points \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (5, 7)\):

$$d_{\text{Manhattan}}(A, B) = |5 - 2| + |7 - 3| = 3 + 4 = 7$$

Notice that while Euclidean distance is 5, Manhattan distance is 7. Because Manhattan navigates orthogonal stair-steps, it is always greater than or equal to Euclidean distance (\(d_{\text{Manhattan}} \ge d_{\text{Euclidean}}\)).

00:25:20 to 00:29:04Slides 16–19

Euclidean vs. Manhattan comparison

Dr. Bulut contrasts when to select each metric:

Euclidean Distance (\(L_2\))

00:26:12 Straight line across open space. Natural for physical coordinates, continuous spatial geometries, robotics, and image pixels. Squares errors, making it sensitive to extreme coordinate outliers.

Manhattan Distance (\(L_1\))

00:28:44 Grid-based movement. Natural for city street layouts, integrated circuit routing, and high-dimensional sparse spaces (text). Sums absolute differences without squaring, making it more robust to single large feature anomalies.

Minkowski, Chebyshev, and Mahalanobis Spaces

The generalized Lp parameter family, chessboard maximum coordinate differences, and scale-aware elliptical covariance contours.

00:29:04 to 00:36:02Slides 20–25

Minkowski and Chebyshev distances

Minkowski Distance is the overarching generalized metric family parameterized by exponent \(p\):

$$d(x, y) = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^p \right)^{1/p}$$

The Minkowski Metric Continuum

p = 1 (Manhattan L1)p = 2 (Euclidean L2)p → ∞ (Chebyshev L∞)

Chebyshev Distance (\(p \to \infty\), also called Chessboard distance) measures distance strictly by the single largest coordinate difference: \(d_\infty(x, y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|\). In chess, a king moving diagonally or orthogonally takes exactly \(\max(\Delta x, \Delta y)\) moves to reach any target square.

00:36:02 to 00:48:20Slides 26–32

Mahalanobis distance: covariance-aware geometry

Dr. Bulut asks: why can Euclidean distance be dangerously misleading? Euclidean distance treats all feature axes as independent and equally scaled, forming circular spherical distance contours. But what if Feature 1 and Feature 2 are highly correlated (e.g., Height and Weight)?

Mahalanobis Distance accounts for feature variance and inter-feature covariance: \(d_M(x, y) = \sqrt{(x - y)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - y)}\), where \(\Sigma\) is the covariance matrix. Rather than rigid circles, equal Mahalanobis distance contours form ellipses stretched along the data trend. A point far along the correlation axis is mathematically closer than an outlier lying off the trend line.

EXAM RADAR UPDATE Week 6 Clarification · 00:02:20

Mahalanobis Formula Exam Scope: In the Week 6 retrospective lecture, Dr. Bulut explicitly reassured students that the Mahalanobis matrix distance formula is not tested by manual calculation on the exam (no covariance matrix inversion required). Students are only expected to understand its conceptual principle: measuring distance in units of standard deviation along correlated axes.

k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Foundations

Rousseau’s social maxim, the hadith of companionship, majority voting mechanics, and lazy instance-based classification.

00:48:20 to 01:01:40Slides 33–36

The philosophical intuition of k-NN

Dr. Bulut introduces the intuition behind K-Nearest Neighbors through classic wisdom. He quotes Genevan philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau: “Tell me who your friends are, and I will tell you who you are.” If your circle consists of scholars, you will adopt scholarship; if your friends are billionaires, you will become the sixth.

He connects this to the prophetic hadith of Hz. Muhammad (PBUH): “الْمَرْءُ مَعَ مَنْ أَحَب” (A person will be with whom he loves). In data science, an unknown instance is classified according to the identity of its closest companions in feature space.

Dr. Bulut summarizes the complete algorithm: To classify a new query point, find the k closest labeled training instances in feature space and assign the class that wins the majority vote.

The Voting Mechanism: Odd k and Bias–Variance

Why even k values risk deadlock ties, small k sensitivity to outliers, and large k majority class domination.

01:26:40 to 01:30:20Slides 61–64

Why is k almost always an odd number?

Dr. Bulut asks: “Why do we usually prefer an odd k?” In binary classification, if you pick an even integer like \(k = 4\), the nearest neighbors could easily produce 2 Yes and 2 No → a 50/50 deadlock tie! The algorithm cannot make an objective prediction. Choosing odd integers (\(k = 1, 3, 5, 7\)) mathematically guarantees an unshakeable majority winner.

Small k (e.g. k = 1)

01:27:16 Hyper-local & flexible. Captures fine-grained local islands. However, it has high variance: extremely sensitive to noise and mislabeled outliers. A single noisy point alters predictions.

Large k (e.g. k = 25)

01:28:02 Smooth & robust. Averages out noise and forms smooth boundaries. However, it has high bias: if one class is more numerous, large \(k\) neighbors will swamp local minorities.

Voronoi Diagrams: 1-NN and Nature’s Tessellations

Perpendicular bisectors dividing space, Voronoi cells in nature, cellular tower zoning, and continuous k-NN regression averages.

01:30:20 to 01:35:39Slides 65–75

1-NN is a Voronoi diagram

When \(k = 1\), what does the decision boundary look like? Dr. Bulut reveals that 1-NN classification produces an exact Voronoi Tessellation. Every training point acts as a seed generating a polygonal cell; any query landing within that polygon is closer to that seed than to any other training instance.

He illustrates how Voronoi geometry governs the natural and built world: the vascular venation of plant leaves, the mosaic patterns on giraffe hides, dragonfly wing membranes, and modern telecommunication tower service cells.

Next, he presents k-NN Regression: instead of taking a discrete majority vote, the algorithm computes the continuous average (mean) of the \(k\) nearest neighbors’ target values (\(\hat{y} = \frac{1}{k}\sum y_i\)). For a house query with three neighbors priced at $210k, $225k, and $240k, the prediction is \(\frac{210 + 225 + 240}{3} = \$225\text{k}\).

Linear Regression, OLS, and Exam Practice

Fitting global straight lines through clouds of points, minimizing squared residuals, interpolation limits, and exam-style calculation walkthroughs.

01:35:39 to 01:40:31Slides 76–89

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression

Dr. Bulut transitions from local non-parametric neighbors to global parametric modelling: Linear Regression. The model assumes a straight-line trend: \(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), where \(\beta_0\) is the intercept and \(\beta_1\) is the slope.

The optimal line is discovered via Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), which minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals: \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\). He presents the Height vs. Weight study (20 patients), fitting the equation \(\text{Weight} = -90 + 0.90 \times \text{Height}\). A person with height 170 cm has a predicted weight of \(-90 + 0.90(170) = 63\text{ kg}\).

Interpolation

01:38:40 Predicting within the observed domain of training instances (e.g. Heights 150–190 cm). Highly reliable.

Extrapolation

01:39:15 Predicting far beyond observed boundaries (e.g. Height 240 cm or 50 cm). Extremely risky; assumes linear trends hold infinitely.
