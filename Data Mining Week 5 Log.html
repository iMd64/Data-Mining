The Geometry of Distance and Euclidean Metric

Distance as mathematical dissimilarity, Pythagoras in feature space, straight-line distance, and multi-dimensional coordinate separation.

00:00:00 to 00:16:11Slides 1–7

Distance equals dissimilarity

Dr. Faruk Bulut opens Week 5 by connecting attribute values to physical geometry. In data mining, distance is the formal mathematical representation of dissimilarity. When two instances sit close together in feature space, they share similar traits; when they are separated by large distances, they behave dissimilarly.

He poses the core question with two points \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (7, 6)\): how far apart are they? The answer depends entirely on the geometric metric chosen to measure the space.

00:16:11 to 00:20:22Slides 8–12

The Euclidean metric (\(L_2\) Norm)

The most familiar metric is Euclidean Distance—the ordinary straight-line distance between two points, derived directly from the Pythagorean theorem:

$$d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}$$

In two dimensions, for \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (5, 7)\):

$$d(A, B) = \sqrt{(5 - 2)^2 + (7 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5.0$$

He extends the formula to 3D feature spaces: \(A = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(B = (4, 6, 3) \implies d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = 5.0\). Euclidean distance represents the ruler distance across continuous space.

Manhattan, City-Blocks, and Grid Navigation

Moving along orthogonal grids, city block taxi navigation, L1 norm calculations, and comparing straight-line vs perimeter paths.

00:20:22 to 00:25:20Slides 13–15

Manhattan distance (\(L_1\) Norm)

If you are a taxi driver in Manhattan, you cannot drive through skyscrapers along a straight diagonal line. You must travel strictly along the horizontal streets and vertical avenues. This is Manhattan Distance (also known as City-Block or Taxicab distance):

$$d(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|$$

For the same two points \(A = (2, 3)\) and \(B = (5, 7)\):

$$d_{\text{Manhattan}}(A, B) = |5 - 2| + |7 - 3| = 3 + 4 = 7$$

Notice that while Euclidean distance is 5, Manhattan distance is 7. Because Manhattan navigates orthogonal stair-steps, it is always greater than or equal to Euclidean distance (\(d_{\text{Manhattan}} \ge d_{\text{Euclidean}}\)).

00:25:20 to 00:29:04Slides 16–19

Euclidean vs. Manhattan comparison

Dr. Bulut contrasts when to select each metric:

Euclidean Distance (\(L_2\))

00:26:12 Straight line across open space. Natural for physical coordinates, continuous spatial geometries, robotics, and image pixels. Squares errors, making it sensitive to extreme coordinate outliers.

Manhattan Distance (\(L_1\))

00:28:44 Grid-based movement. Natural for city street layouts, integrated circuit routing, and high-dimensional sparse spaces (text). Sums absolute differences without squaring, making it more robust to single large feature anomalies.

Minkowski, Chebyshev, and Mahalanobis Spaces

The generalized \(L_p\) parameter family, Chebyshev chessboard infinity metric, covariance-aware Mahalanobis geometry, specialized distance metrics, and the mid-lecture intermission.

00:29:04to 00:36:02Slides 20–25

Minkowski and Chebyshev distances

Dr. Bulut transitions from rigid 2D grids to the generalized Minkowski Distance (\(L_p\) Norm): \(d_p(x, y) = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i - y_i|^p \right)^{1/p}\). He illustrates how varying the exponent \(p\) morphs the geometric space: \(p = 1\) yields Manhattan distance, \(p = 2\) yields Euclidean distance, and as \(p \to \infty\), the formula converges to the Chebyshev Distance (\(L_\infty\) Norm): \(d_\infty(x, y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|\).

In chessboard geometry, Chebyshev distance represents the exact move cost of a King, which can step horizontally, vertically, or diagonally in a single move. In high-dimensional data spaces, Chebyshev measures the single worst-case coordinate divergence between two entities.

00:36:02to 00:54:30Slides 26–32

Mahalanobis distance & specialized metrics (Cosine, Hamming, Haversine)

Dr. Bulut asks: why can Euclidean distance be dangerously misleading? Euclidean distance treats all feature axes as independent and equally scaled, forming circular spherical distance contours. But what if Feature 1 and Feature 2 are highly correlated (e.g., Height and Weight)?

Mahalanobis Distance accounts for feature variance and inter-feature covariance: \(d_M(x, y) = \sqrt{(x - y)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - y)}\), where \(\Sigma\) is the covariance matrix. Rather than rigid circles, equal Mahalanobis distance contours form ellipses stretched along the data trend. A point far along the correlation axis is mathematically closer than an outlier lying off the trend line.

Cosine Distance / Similarity

00:50:45 Measures the angular orientation between feature vectors rather than length: \(1 - \cos(\theta) = 1 - \frac{A \cdot B}{\|A\| \|B\|}\). Essential for text mining and document frequency where document word counts vary dramatically.

Hamming & Haversine Metrics

00:52:10 Hamming Distance: Counts character or bit mismatches across strings (inapplicable to continuous geometric space).
Haversine Metric: Calculates great-circle spherical distance across Earth's surface for GPS and navigational coordinates.

k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Foundations

Rousseau’s social maxim, the hadith of companionship, binary vs. multi-class classification, hyperparameter \(k\), the odd \(k\) voting heuristic, and instance-based lazy learning.

00:56:30to 01:06:00Slides 33–38

The philosophical intuition of k-NN & classification taxonomy

At 00:56:30, Dr. Bulut introduces the intuition behind K-Nearest Neighbors through classic wisdom. He quotes Genevan philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau: “Tell me who your friends are, and I will tell you who you are.” If your circle consists of scholars, you will adopt scholarship; if your friends are billionaires, you will become the sixth.

He connects this to the prophetic hadith of Hz. Muhammad (PBUH): “الْمَرْءُ مَعَ مَنْ أَحَب” (A person will be with whom he loves). In data science, an unknown instance is classified according to the identity of its closest companions in feature space.

Classification is defined as a supervised task predicting a categorical class label. He contrasts Binary Classification (two outcomes, e.g., Yes/No, Defective/Functional) with Multi-Class Classification (three or more categories), demonstrating how nearest neighbor query circles expand across 2D and 3D Euclidean feature spaces to locate the closest candidate points.

01:06:00to 01:15:46Slides 39–46

Hyperparameter \(k\), Odd vs. Even \(k\), and Lazy Learning

Dr. Bulut emphasizes that \(k\) is an outer hyperparameter—a parameter specified by the user from outside the algorithm, not learned automatically from training data. How should \(k\) be chosen?

The Odd \(k\) Rule: If \(k\) is an even number in binary classification (e.g., \(k = 4\)), the nearest neighbors may yield a tie (2 for Class A, 2 for Class B), leaving the classifier unable to reach a majority verdict. Selecting an odd value of \(k\) (\(k = 3, 5, 7\)) mathematically guarantees an outright majority winner in two-class problems.

In one summarizing sentence, he crystallizes why k-NN is unique: k-NN is Supervised and Lazy. It is supervised because training points possess ground-truth class labels. It is lazy (instance-based) because it constructs no global hypothesis or predictive model in advance; it simply stores the dataset in memory and defers all distance calculations until a concrete query point arrives.

Supervised Aspect

01:12:31 Training points are pre-labeled with historical ground-truth classes (e.g. Heart Attack: Yes/No).

Lazy Aspect

01:12:59 Zero training time model construction. All computation occurs at query time when distances to every training instance are computed.

The Voting Mechanism: Odd k and Bias–Variance

Why even k values risk deadlock ties, small k sensitivity to outliers, and large k majority class domination.

01:26:40 to 01:30:20Slides 61–64

Why is k almost always an odd number?

Dr. Bulut asks: “Why do we usually prefer an odd k?” In binary classification, if you pick an even integer like \(k = 4\), the nearest neighbors could easily produce 2 Yes and 2 No → a 50/50 deadlock tie! The algorithm cannot make an objective prediction. Choosing odd integers (\(k = 1, 3, 5, 7\)) mathematically guarantees an unshakeable majority winner.

Small k (e.g. k = 1)

01:27:16 Hyper-local & flexible. Captures fine-grained local islands. However, it has high variance: extremely sensitive to noise and mislabeled outliers. A single noisy point alters predictions.

Large k (e.g. k = 25)

01:28:02 Smooth & robust. Averages out noise and forms smooth boundaries. However, it has high bias: if one class is more numerous, large \(k\) neighbors will swamp local minorities.

Voronoi Diagrams: 1-NN and Nature’s Tessellations

Perpendicular bisectors dividing space into nearest-neighbor Dirichlet polygons, and the pervasive appearance of Voronoi tessellations across biology and physics.

01:30:20to 01:35:08Slides 65–75

1-NN is a Voronoi diagram & Nature’s Tessellations

When \(k = 1\), what does the decision boundary look like? Dr. Bulut reveals that 1-NN classification produces an exact Voronoi Tessellation. Every training point acts as a seed generating a polygonal cell; any query landing within that polygon is closer to that seed than to any other training instance.

He illustrates how Voronoi geometry governs the natural and built world: the vascular venation of plant leaves, the mosaic patterns on giraffe hides, dragonfly wing membranes, and modern telecommunication tower service cells.

k-NN Regression, Algorithmic Reality, Exam Practice & OLS Scope

Continuous target prediction via neighbor averaging, algorithm strengths and computational scaling limits, pseudocode, the Point Q exam exercise, and Linear Regression scope clarification.

01:35:08to 01:39:13Slides 76–82

k-NN Classification vs. Regression, Strengths & Limitations

At 01:35:08, Dr. Bulut draws a fundamental methodological distinction between k-NN Classification and k-NN Regression:

k-NN Classification

01:35:39 Predicts a discrete categorical class label using majority voting (e.g. Play Tennis: Yes/No, Loan: Approve/Deny).

k-NN Regression

01:35:59 Predicts a continuous real numerical target by computing the arithmetic mean (average) of the \(k\) nearest neighbors’ values: \(\hat{y} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k y_i\).

He walks through a concrete Real Estate Valuation Example: predicting the market price of a query house based on \(k = 3\) nearest neighbor properties (matched on room count, location, square footage, and view). If the three closest properties sold for $210k, $225k, and $240k, k-NN regression estimates the query house value as \(\frac{210 + 225 + 240}{3} = \mathbf{\$225\text{k}}\).

He analyzes the algorithm’s prominent Strengths and Limitations (pros & cons):

  • Strengths: Highly intuitive, transparent, non-parametric (no rigid distributional assumptions), and effortlessly forms flexible non-linear decision boundaries.
  • Limitations: Computationally slow during the prediction phase on large datasets. If a dataset contains 1,000,000 instances, evaluating a single query requires computing 1,000,000 Euclidean distance evaluations (\(O(N)\) complexity). Furthermore, it is highly sensitive to feature scaling and noisy, irrelevant attributes.
